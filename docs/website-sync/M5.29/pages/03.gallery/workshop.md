<h2 id="photographs" style="scroll-margin-top:6rem">The kit that caught my eye</h2>

<figure>
  <a href="/projects/pidp-11/pidp11.jpg"><img src="/projects/pidp-11/pidp11.jpg" alt="PiDP-11/70 replica kit in an Obsolescence Guaranteed reference photograph, not a photograph of my assembled kit" loading="lazy" width="907" height="605"></a>
  <figcaption>Reference photograph from the <a href="https://obsolescence.dev/pdp11.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Obsolescence Guaranteed</a> website, not a photograph of my own build. The PiDP-11 is built at a 6:10 scale (60% of the original DEC PDP-11/70 size). The PDP-11/70 was a multi-user, time-sharing minicomputer (though it could still fill a room). Instead of sitting at the machine itself with a monitor and keyboard, dozens of people sat at "dumb terminals" (like the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VT100" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DEC VT100</a>) connected via serial cables or modems. The PDP-11 rapidly switched its processor time between users, giving each person the illusion they had the computer to themselves.</figcaption>
</figure>
